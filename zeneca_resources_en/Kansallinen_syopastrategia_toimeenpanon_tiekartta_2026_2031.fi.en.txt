Summary
National Cancer Strategy 2026–2035: 
Implementation roadmap for 2026–2031
The roadmap for the implementation of the National Cancer Strategy guides the phased 
implementation of the strategy in the first five-year period 2026–2031. The aim is to develop 
people-oriented services, reduce the cancer burden through prevention and early detection, 
and ensure effective and equal treatment. Although cancer treatment in Finland is of a high 
standard, the growing cancer burden and the strategy’s goal of achieving treatment outcomes 
at the Nordic level require significant reforms. The personalization of diagnostics and 
treatment creates new opportunities, but also increases the need to strengthen the 
knowledge base and improve the effectiveness of cancer prevention, treatment and research.
The roadmap describes the measures, their timetables, responsible actors and resource 
needs. The goal of the roadmap is to ensure the practical implementation of the strategy and 
clarify the roles of different administrative sectors, authorities, welfare areas and 
organizations. Success requires broad national commitment and active policy actions.
Some of the measures are already underway as part of broader national projects, but 
the implementation of several measures requires additional resources and legislative 
changes. The implementation of the Cancer Strategy requires an estimated additional 
investment of EUR 140 million in total, which is expected to produce significant savings 
and growth through prevented cancer cases, earlier detection and increased research, 
development and innovation.
A monitoring group will be established to coordinate, guide and monitor the implementation of 
the strategy. The roadmap will be updated during implementation to take into account changes in 
the operating environment, and a broader assessment and roadmap for the second five-year period 
will be made in 2030.

Abstract
Finland's National Cancer Strategy 2026–2035: 
Roadmap for implementation for 2026–2031
The roadmap for the implementation of the National Cancer Strategy guides the gradual 
implementation of the strategy during its first five-year period (2026–2031). The aim is to 
develop human-centered services, reduce the cancer burden through prevention and 
early detection, and ensure effective and equal treatment. Although cancer treatment is 
of a high standard in Finland, the growing cancer burden and the goal set in the strategy 
for Finland to be at the same level in treatment results as other Nordic countries will 
require significant reforms. The individualization of diagnostics and treatment creates 
new opportunities, but these also increase the need to strengthen the knowledge base 
and improve the effectiveness of cancer prevention, treatment, and research.
The roadmap describes measures, their schedules, responsible actors, and resource 
needs. The objective of the roadmap is to ensure the practical implementation of the 
strategy and to clarify the roles of different administrative branches, authorities, well-
being services counties, and organizations. Success will require extensive national 
commitment and active policy measures.
Some of the measures are already under way as part of more wide-ranging national 
projects, but the implementation of many measures will require additional resources and 
legislative amendments. The implementation of Finland's National Cancer Strategy as a 
whole will require an estimated EUR 140 million in additional investments, which are 
expected to generate significant savings and growth through prevented cancer cases, 
earlier detection and increased research, development, and innovation activities.
A monitoring group will be set up to coordinate, outline, and monitor the 
implementation of the strategy. The roadmap will be updated during the 
implementation, taking into account changes taking place in the operating 
environment, and a more extensive assessment and a roadmap for the second 
five-year period will be drafted in 2030.

Introduction
In November 2025, the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health (STM) published Finland's first national 
cancer strategy for 2026–2035.1to respond to the challenges of the growing cancer burden and the 
changing operating environment. The strategy aims to develop people-oriented services, reduce the 
cancer burden through prevention and early detection, and ensure equal, prompt and effective 
cancer diagnostics, treatment and rehabilitation throughout the country. The strategy also 
emphasizes the importance of research, new methods and knowledge-based management in the 
development of cancer prevention and treatment.
The strategy outlines long-term actions and presents key priorities for the 
development of cancer prevention, treatment and cancer research until 2035. The 
strategy consists of four strategic main objectives (Figure 1), sub-objectives specified 
under them, and proposed measures to support their implementation. The cross-cutting 
theme of the strategy is equality.
This roadmap for the implementation of the national cancer strategy describes the first concrete 
steps in the implementation of the strategy for the years 2026–2031. The roadmap specifies the 
proposed measures presented in the strategy by describing their practical implementation, 
responsibilities, necessary resources and the timetable for the first five-year period. The goal of the 
roadmap is to enable the planned and phased implementation of the strategy and to support 
guidance and monitoring. The roadmap will be updated as implementation progresses, and the 
intention is to carry out a broader evaluation halfway through the strategy, based on which the 
roadmap for the second half of the strategy period will be determined.

Equality perspectives in the 
implementation of the cancer strategy
The central goal of the Finnish healthcare system is to provide citizens with equal access to 
preventive services and timely, high-quality care.
However, long-term studies show that the risk of developing cancer and cancer 
mortality vary between different population groups. Socioeconomic factors affect both 
cancer incidence, treatment outcomes and relative survival, and there is also great 
regional variation in these factors.
Cancer affects a person's everyday life and life in many ways. The disease and its 
treatments can impair work and functional capacity, quality of life, coping at work and 
livelihood. In addition, late effects of treatments may only become apparent years later 
and recovery may be a long process. Experiences of accessing treatment and the 
functioning of healthcare vary considerably, both between population groups and among 
people with different cancers. Ensuring equality is also important between people with 
cancer and the rest of the population, for example in working life and when applying for 
financial services.
Equality is a fundamental right enshrined in the Finnish Constitution. Formal equality 
means that everyone is treated the same way and provided with the same services. 
However, it is not enough alone to guarantee true equality. Genuine equality for people 
with cancer requires comprehensive and fair consideration of individual life situations, 
sufficient information about available support services, and smooth multi-professional 
cooperation. Only then will everyone have a real chance of achieving the best possible 
treatment outcome.
Not everything for everyone, but to each person according to their individual needs.
Socioeconomic factors
Finnish long-term studies2have shown that the risk of developing cancer and cancer 
mortality vary across population groups when socioeconomic background variables are 
assessed. In addition, individual differences in health literacy, information acquisition 
and interpretation affect treatment and medication adherence.
These differences have been shown in lung, colorectal, breast and prostate cancer, 
among others. In international studies, blood and lymph node cancers

In a study, low socioeconomic status was associated with poorer prognosis in several 
types of cancer, including multiple myeloma, acute leukemia, and non-Hodgkin 
lymphoma. Differences in survival were also observed in children in a Finnish study that 
controlled for parental income, education, and ethnicity.3.
Socioeconomic status is a general concept that cannot be measured directly in studies 
using a single variable. According to the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare, 
socioeconomic status describes the totality of an individual's material resources and the 
conditions required to acquire them. These material resources include income, property and 
standard of living. The conditions for acquiring them include education, occupation and 
position in working life.
There are many possible mechanisms involved in poorer outcomes in patients of low 
socioeconomic status, and the differences are likely due to a combination of several 
factors.
People with low education, low incomes and working in white-collar occupations are 
more likely to get sick, their functional capacity is more often impaired and they die 
younger than highly educated, well-off and senior white-collar workers. The underlying 
factors behind low socioeconomic status and poorer cancer survival are suspicion of 
delayed diagnosis, reduced access to treatment, lower intensity of treatment and lower 
participation in cancer screening. Other factors include lifestyle differences related to 
socioeconomic status in smoking, alcohol consumption and overweight, all of which are 
risk factors for several underlying diseases.
Other identified factors that increase inequalities linked to the healthcare service 
system include the cost of services, differences in patients’ health literacy, the challenging 
location of services and the difficulty of reaching services. For people on low and near-zero 
incomes, current customer fees, as well as medication and travel costs, may be too high, 
which may delay seeking health services or lead to patients saving on medication costs by 
not taking some of their medications. Access to public dental care may also be 
challenging. Occupational healthcare services are free at the point of use, while in public 
healthcare, patients pay customer fees for the majority of services.
People with higher education levels tend to be better informed about available services 
and their rights to use them, which is also reflected in their willingness to use digital 
services. It has been found that seeking care is prolonged if services are located far away 
or difficult to access. Skipping health check-ups and preventive services (e.g. vaccinations, 
age checks and cancer screenings) affects cancer detection and treatment outcomes. In 
addition, those from a lower socio-economic background may face challenges in accessing 
services and may not be confident in receiving help. Negative attitudes among healthcare 
professionals may further complicate the situation.

The increase in the proportion of immigrants in the population also affects cancer 
incidence. Finland's population structure has changed significantly over the past 30 years 
and is still changing. At the end of 2023, just under seven percent of the total population 
was born outside Finland. In Uusimaa, one in ten people was born outside Finland. The 
proportion of immigrants in the working-age population (20–64 years) was 13.3% in the 
whole country and 21.6% in Uusimaa.
As the immigrant population ages, the incidence of cancer in this population group will also 
increase. Those who have arrived from elsewhere may develop different cancers than the native 
population. The most common types of cancer associated with unhealthy lifestyles, i.e. so-called 
lifestyle cancers such as breast and bowel cancer, are less common among immigrants. On the other 
hand, infectious-related cancers – which have become rare in Finland for various reasons – such as 
cervical cancer may be more common among immigrants and lead to more deaths. It can be 
predicted that population change will also be reflected in changes in the burden of disease.
The participation of immigrants in cancer screening is lower than that of those born in Finland. 
There are several reasons for this lack of participation. At the same time, it is worth remembering that 
immigrants or people with foreign languages   do not form a clear and uniform group: there are large 
differences between and within different groups.
Socioeconomic disparities are partly influenced by the Finnish healthcare system, where 
primary healthcare is built on three parallel actors: public healthcare centres managed by 
welfare regions, private healthcare centres and occupational healthcare. Although all are 
financed to varying degrees by public funds, the current situation is that the latter are well 
resourced and access to care is fast, while the former is severely under-resourced and 
faces challenges in accessing care. An as-yet-unpublished study on access to care for 
lymphoma patients found that it takes twice as long to receive a diagnosis compared to 
private actors if a patient presents with symptoms to public healthcare.
When we also take into account that the most comprehensive occupational health care is provided 
to those who are otherwise well-off, the socioeconomic differences in health become even more acute. 
The problem concerns not only diagnostics but also cancer prevention and post-treatment 
rehabilitation.
Well-being and health should be promoted in municipalities, welfare areas and 
organisations, both at the population level and targeted at different groups, and the 
message should be carried forward in cooperation with different parties. Building trust 
and raising awareness requires time and resources, and results will only be seen years 
later.
There is no reason to perceive different minority groups as a group requiring special 
services and thus resources. We serve everyone better when we take minority groups into 
account. It is worth increasing the use of plain Finnish in communication. Anything that helps 
to reach as many minority groups as possible is also beneficial to others.

Regional inequality
Cancer treatment outcomes measure the effectiveness of the entire healthcare system: 
cancer prevention, access to screening and primary care, prompt diagnostics and a care 
chain that combines specialized medical specialties. The goal is to provide equal 
healthcare services to everyone, regardless of place of residence and socioeconomic 
background. Regional equality has improved in Finland in recent decades4. However, there 
is still great regional variation in cancer incidence, treatment outcomes and relative 
survival.
Studies show that people living far from hospitals/rural areas are diagnosed with 
cancer at a later stage on average. In remote areas, access to primary health care services 
is poorer, the population has lower health literacy, and the rate of seeking treatment is 
slower than in cities.5. Different exposures to cancer risk factors, such as radon, asbestos, 
tobacco, and lifestyle and health behavior-related exposures, may also contribute to 
regional differences in cancer incidence.6.7.
The path of a cancer patient from primary care to further examinations and specialist 
care is complex and always involves several healthcare contacts and examinations. There 
is currently a large regional variation in the time from the first symptom of cancer to 
diagnosis and initiation of treatment, which also depends on the route of

Cancer treatment always requires specialized expertise, and centralizing treatment in 
centers of expertise has been shown to improve cancer treatment outcomes. The results of 
demanding cancer surgery in particular are best in high-volume centers. With the 
Centralization Decree (582/2017), most cancer surgery is performed in university hospitals. 
Also, demanding cancer drug treatment, cell therapy treatments, radiotherapy and clinical 
drug trials have been centralized in university hospitals and the largest central hospitals. 
Finland differs from most European countries in terms of sparse population, long distances 
and poor public transport. For some of the population, the distance to the nearest central 
hospital is over 400 km and to the nearest university hospital is over 700 km. Distance reduces 
the ability of patients living far from the hospital to receive long-term and frequent hospital 
visits and to commit to such treatment.
The use of palliative radiotherapy has been shown to be lower in patients who live more 
than 100 km from the nearest radiotherapy unit.8, and treatment outcomes are poorer if the 
patient lives more than 50 km away from the treatment unit9. Distance to the treatment unit 
can also affect other treatment decisions. For example, a surgical technique for breast cancer 
may be chosen to be more extensive than usual in order to avoid travel caused by 
radiotherapy, or a patient may want a treatment that may have been less effective in studies if 
it can be carried out at home or close to home. In order to guarantee regionally equal 
treatment –   and on the other hand to control travel costs – in addition to centralisation, it is 
necessary to ensure that the hospital network is sufficiently dense. In addition, care must be 
taken to ensure that treatments that can be carried out in a decentralised manner, for example 
by utilising remote services or by increasing cooperation between primary and specialist health 
care, are provided as close as possible to the patient’s home.10Palliative care for cancer patients 
living in sparsely populated areas and far from cities should also be available at home or as 
close to home as possible through a home hospital.
Cancer treatment practices vary by region, and the same cancer can be treated differently 
depending on the patient's chosen treatment location. National and international cancer 
treatment guidelines improve treatment equity and outcomes11also from the perspective of 
regional equality. Treatment recommendations enable a sufficient level of expertise in cancer 
treatment and continuous updating of knowledge, also in central hospitals, where the doctor 
responsible for cancer treatment must have basic knowledge of all the most common types of 
cancer.

Elderly
In 2022, 37,268 new cancer cases were diagnosed in Finland, and in 2040, the number of cases is 
estimated to be around 48,500. The increase is mainly due to the aging of the population. The number of 
cancers diagnosed in people over 75 years of age will increase by more than 60% by 2040, and one in five 
new cancers will be diagnosed in people over 85 years of age.
Despite the large number of elderly cancer patients, elderly people are 
underrepresented in clinical trials, which is why scientific evidence on the tolerability and 
efficacy of cancer drugs in elderly patients is lacking.12Although healthy elderly people 
benefit from cancer treatments, for some, frailty, geriatric syndromes, and other illnesses 
weaken their resources to such an extent that the harms of treatment may outweigh the 
benefits.
Cancer diagnostics, treatment and monitoring are disease-centred, and the 
assessment of the functional capacity of the elderly patient and their participation in 
decision-making are often inadequate. Patients may be referred for invasive diagnostic, 
therapeutic and monitoring procedures without identifying frail and/or multimorbid 
patients in whom such procedures would require clinical assessment. Invasive 
examinations, such as colonoscopy with emptying, can be harmful and unethical for frail 
patients, and may not be effective.

National cancer-specific treatment recommendations should guide the assessment of the overall 
situation of the elderly patient. Information on functional capacity should guide the cancer

the patient may not be able to tolerate heavy cancer treatments, but will benefit more from 
referral to palliative care.
Finland needs national guidance in geriatric oncology at the cancer center level. The 
national geriatric oncology society supports operations and personnel training and 
collaborates with international societies. The society participates in making cancer-specific 
treatment recommendations and ensures that the elderly are taken into account in them.

Cancers in children and adolescents
In Finland, approximately 200 people under the age of 18 develop cancer each year. The diagnostics, 
treatment planning and mainly the implementation of cancer in children and adolescents are centralized 
in university hospitals. Allogeneic stem cell transplants for children are centralized nationally in HUS, from 
which the responsibility for treatment returns to the university hospital. At the end of the treatment, the 
university hospital plans the patient's individual follow-up, which is implemented in cooperation with the 
central hospitals. A significant proportion of those who receive cancer treatment during their growing 
years develop late side effects, which is why a treatment summary and adult follow-up recommendation 
are prepared for all patients at the university hospital.13Each unit providing cancer treatment monitors its 
own activities and can compare them to the international level using the childhood cancer quality registry 
tool (BCB-Medical) and international treatment studies.
Childhood and adolescent cancers are rare diseases, and the treatment of patients in 
each diagnostic group (International Classification of Childhood Cancer) is carried out in 
accordance with international treatment guidelines. Patients are mainly treated in 
international academic clinical treatment trials, the majority of which are coordinated by a 
European consortium, SIOPE (The European Society for Paediatric Oncology).14. Risk 
classification, diagnostics (including molecular genetic studies) and structured follow-up 
are carried out according to the guidelines of these treatment studies. Treatment studies 
are mainly carried out with foundation funding and donations. In addition, stem cell 
transplantation activities are required to maintain and document accreditation status. 
Finnish treatment outcomes are at a high level thanks to the established operating model

and reduced late effects. It is important to monitor these effects also in adulthood, when

Rare cancers
Cancer is classified as rare if the number of new cases is less than 6 per 100,000 people 
per year. Rare cancers occur in all age groups and affect all organ systems. About one fifth 
of cancer patients have the disease classified as rare, meaning that approximately 7,000 
people in Finland develop rare cancers each year. The challenges of rare cancers are 
diverse and partly different for different cancers.
Symptoms that differ from typical cancer or vague symptoms delay access to 
treatment, especially when cancer is diagnosed at a young age. Diagnostics may require 
special methods and additional tests, which the professional must be able to suspect a 
rare cancer in order to perform. A significant proportion of patients visit many doctors 
before receiving the correct diagnosis. In addition, many patients initially receive one or 
more incorrect diagnoses. The specialized pathology and genetics expertise required for a 
correct diagnosis is also not available in all welfare areas.

Once a diagnosis has been made, the lack of treatment recommendations or the difficulty in 
obtaining effective medication for the patient can make it difficult to implement the treatment. In 
particular, targeted drug therapies are known to be more effective in rare disease diagnoses. 
Different types of cancer in cancer syndromes may also share a common molecular mechanism that 
would be effective regardless of the cancer type. However, there is often a lack of a decision on the 
reimbursability of a potential drug, and effective treatment would have to be given outside the 
official indication (so-called off-label).
People with rare cancers also find it more difficult than others to find information 
about their disease, or information about the disease is not available. In addition, 
patients are more likely to lack peer support and are therefore more vulnerable to 
psychological and social problems. Although rare cancers are diverse and different 
diseases, many of the challenges arising from their rarity unite them. To solve the 
challenges, rare cancers should be treated as a whole, and functional solutions must be 
designed for the identified problem areas.
In recent years, national cooperation has been developed in Finland to improve the 
treatment of rare cancers and cancer susceptibility syndromes in a national project 
coordinated by FICAN South and FICAN Mid. In the development work, key challenges 
identified include delays in diagnosis, clarity of the overall treatment pathway, consistency 
of monitoring, and availability of expert expertise and medicines. In addition, referral to 
third sector services is inadequate. Resolving these challenges requires nationally 
coordinated operating models to support the diagnostics, treatment, and monitoring of 
rare cancers in the future.
Finnish university hospitals are members of the following European networks for rare 
cancers: ERN EURACAN (HUS, Tays, Tyks), ERN EuroBloodNet (HUS), ERN Genturis (HUS,

colleagues can be consulted if necessary and the knowledge and expertise of a large

Cancers with a poor prognosis
In Finland, cancer patient survival has improved significantly, and our country is at the top of 
the international cancer treatment scale. However, certain cancers, such as pancreatic, liver, 
gallbladder, bile duct, lung, esophageal and stomach cancers, are still among the cancers with 
a poor prognosis, even internationally. These cancers are often diagnosed at an advanced 
stage, when treatments are no longer curative and the prognosis is significantly worse. The 
effectiveness of different treatment options is often limited, and unfortunately, most patients 
die from cancer.
The symptoms of cancers with a poor prognosis can be vague, which may lead to early 
diagnostic deficiencies and delay access to treatment. For example, stigma associated with 
smoking and lung cancer and the assumption that treatments are ineffective can also delay 
seeking treatment and access to treatment. Some of these cancers are also difficult to detect 
early with current diagnostic methods. In certain cancers, monitoring of risk groups is an 
important part of early diagnosis, and national monitoring recommendations have just been 
published for hereditary pancreatic cancer, for example. Legislative obstacles, such as 
secondary legislation, have made international research collaboration difficult. Only a small 
proportion of patients are treated in clinical treatment trials.

Strengthening research and development
Cancer research plays a key role in improving the prognosis of cancers with poor 
prognosis. The development of other cancer treatments has given hope that more 
effective solutions could also be found for the early detection and treatment of cancers 
with poor prognosis. Researcher-driven research and cooperation with pharmaceutical 
companies expand the research front. Targeting national research funding to research on 
cancers with poor prognosis is important. Removing legislative obstacles will facilitate 
joining international research consortia. Also, resourcing international project and 
research funding applications is a way to enhance international research cooperation. 
The implementation of clinical drug and treatment trials and patient recruitment are 
promoted through sufficient personnel resourcing and smooth processes.
Indicators include research funding received, publications, number of treatment trials 
and patients recruited, and regional coverage. For cancers with poor prognosis, results can 
also be expected to be seen quickly in patient diagnostics, access to treatment, and 
survival.
Care chains and cooperation
The smoothness of the care chain and the cooperation between different professional groups have 
a key impact on the care a patient receives. Multidisciplinary meetings aim to ensure that the 
patient receives the best possible and nationally consistent quality of care. In the treatment of rare 
cancers with poor prognosis, it is important to centralize treatment planning and certain treatment 
procedures in specialized centers.
Quality registers are essential for monitoring the quality and effectiveness of care. 
Updating registers to align with, for example, the Nordic countries can improve data collection 
and comparison of care outcomes.
Tailoring monitoring and treatment to risk groups is also very important. For example, 
in pancreatic cancer, it has been found that CT scans performed every three months 
during the first year after curative surgery can extend survival by up to five months.
Early initiation of palliative care is also an essential part of the treatment of cancers with 
poor prognosis. Palliative care can improve the patient's quality of life and prolong survival. 
Early transition to symptomatic care is in the patient's best interest, as it also avoids 
burdensome treatments and procedures that do not improve the prognosis or quality of life. 
Psychosocial support for the patient and their loved ones is essential throughout the treatment 
process.
Indicators include access to treatment times, cancer prevalence, and indicators 
describing treatment outcomes: the proportion of patients receiving curative treatment 
and survival. Objective indicators enable national and international comparison and 
promote equality.

Measure 2.4.2 Reform legislation to enable cost-effective and 
risk-based screening
The legislation will be updated so that cancer screening can be implemented effectively based 
on defined risk, whereby more frequent or less frequent individual monitoring allocates 
resources as appropriately as possible. The risk assessment must be based on national registry 
data so that the implementation method is uniform throughout Finland. This requires a change 
in legislation so that registry data can be used to define risk and screening can be implemented 
on a risk-based basis. The Ministry of Social Affairs and Health is currently investigating these 
needs for change in connection with the legislative project enabling proactive social and health 
care (STM011:00/2026).
Risk-based screening requires comprehensive and up-to-date information on 
screenings, as well as on non-screening tests and follow-up examinations. Therefore, all 
healthcare providers should be required to submit information on all screening (so-called 
opportunistic) tests to the screening register.
When updating the legislation, unnecessary regulations should be removed. For 
example, the requirement in the Screening Regulation for statements from two 
independent radiologists significantly hinders the use of artificial intelligence in breast 
cancer screening.

Measure 2.4.3 Target screening based on the statistically assessed risk of the 
target population, whereby some of those screened can be invited for screening 
less frequently or more frequently
The aim of risk-based screening is to target national cancer screening programmes in a 
way that maximises the benefits of screening and minimises harms and costs. 
Implementing such screening requires changes to the legislation specified in measure 
2.4.2.
Once the legislative changes have been made, national protocols for implementing 
risk-based screening should be defined in various cancer screening programmes. It should 
be possible to use data from national registries in risk assessment. In the first phase of 
implementation, for example, persons who do not have the organ targeted for screening 
due to a possible hysterectomy or colectomy can be completely removed from the 
population invited for screening, or the screening interval can be extended for those who 
have received the HPV vaccine and have recently participated in screening tests or more 
detailed diagnostic tests. In a later phase, the target population invited for screening can 
be modified based on statistically modelled risk. Health data from national registries can 
be used in risk determination. In this case, the screening interval for persons at low risk 
could be extended or the screening interval for those at high risk could be increased 
accordingly.
In addition, the possibilities of streamlining and automating the delivery of screening information, for 
example by utilizing integrations between registers, should be explored. The reporting obligation 
regarding screenings should be taken into account when launching potential new screening programs, 
and national registers should be established for the programs in the same way as the current screening 
registers. When resourcing screening, the resources required for establishing and maintaining registers 
should always be taken into account.

Cancer care involves several specialties, such as oncology, haematology, pulmonary diseases 
and surgical disciplines including gynecology. The majority of treatment recommendations are

Measure 4.3.2 Create national recommendations on the scope and

Molecular diagnostics of cancer refers to the study of tumor-specific molecular changes 
for targeted therapies. Molecular changes can occur at the DNA, RNA or protein level, and 
there are several methodological options for studying them. Currently, there is no national 
recommendation on which molecular changes should be studied for different cancer

regularly updated national treatment recommendations. The recommendations also 
determine the uniform introduction of new research methods, for example liquid biopsies.

recommendations in use, which determine, among other things, the research target, diagnostic 
method, and reporting framework. The recommendation may also take a position on the availability 
or implementation of services so that each welfare area has the opportunity to use molecular

Measure 4.5.3 Enable the use of real-life efficacy data in the controlled 
introduction of new medicines, the development of risk-sharing models 
and the evaluation of efficacy
The systematic use of real-life effectiveness data (RWE) is important in the introduction of 
new medicines, especially in situations where there is significant uncertainty about the 
effects or costs of a new drug treatment. Managed introduction models can support 
better patient care and enable fair, flexible and dynamic financing solutions. RWE is a key 
part of the modern evaluation and financing system for cancer drugs, as new cancer 
drugs are entering the market faster than before and in some cases with less certain 
evidence of the drug's effectiveness. Therefore, it is appropriate to use real-life 
effectiveness data accumulated in clinical use.
To manage the costs of new cancer drugs to society, models for sharing financial risk 
between pharmaceutical companies and payers are needed. This is closely linked to 
monitoring treatment outcomes, i.e. collecting information on whether the drug works in 
Finnish treatment practices and whether the results correspond to those obtained from 
clinical trials. Additional information may also need to be collected on the impact of 
introduction on the use of services and resources. This information will allow for a more 
accurate cost-effectiveness analysis and more informed and traceable decisions on the 
use of drugs. However, generating RWE alone is not enough; the ability to use the 
information in the management and control of the drug portfolio and in strategic 
procurement is also needed. Contracts can be complex and administratively burdensome, 
and therefore the development of a national operating model and indicators is important.

treatment cannot be a separate solution from other healthcare, so the financial responsibility 
remains with the welfare regions and HUS. Joint procurement will be utilised whenever it is 
possible within the framework of the Procurement Act and competitive advantages can be 
achieved. The national procurement support service could also prepare direct procurement 
contracts for treatment technologies on behalf of the welfare regions. The role of the 
procurement support service would also include anticipating new innovations (horizon 
scanning).
To achieve this goal, a national operating model and responsible body will be created, 
which will discuss and decide on the national need for equipment and methods in the entire 
healthcare system, their procurement and placement in different welfare areas and HUS based 
on the centralization decree. The body can operate in a network-like manner and the Ministry 
of Health, Social Affairs, welfare areas and HUS could participate in the development of the 
operating model, and Palko, the network of chief evaluators, FinCCHTA and FINOSE, which will 
conduct method evaluations for new methods. In the future, the evaluation could be linked to 
investment planning in accordance with the Social and Health Care Organisation Act and the 
division of labour between welfare areas so that equipment investments support effective 
centralization of care and a cost-effective service system. Strengthening and compiling national 
evaluation activities require a legislative project in the implementation phase.

Implementation and monitoring
According to the Cancer Strategy, the implementation of the objectives requires sufficient 
resourcing, clear national-level guidance and strong commitment from various actors. The 
implementation of the objectives of the strategy has been specified in the previous chapters of this 
roadmap in terms of the parties responsible for the measures, schedules, necessary resources, 
anticipated savings and necessary regulatory changes, and in the following chapter in terms of 
costs and savings.
For implementation and monitoring, a national cancer strategy monitoring group will be established, 
which will include representatives from the background organizations of the roadmap steering group 
(FICAN, Cancer Organizations, THL, STM), the welfare regions and HUS, other ministries, authorities, 
universities and organizations important for the implementation of the strategy. The monitoring group's 
key tasks are to plan, monitor and coordinate the implementation of the national cancer strategy and its 
phasing, guided by the strategy, roadmap and operating environment (including the EU Cancer Mission 
and the Cancer Control Plan), and to propose legislative changes and funding opportunities to promote 
implementation. The activities of the monitoring group and the implementation of the strategy will be 
coordinated by FICAN within the framework of available resources.
The activities of the Finnish Cancer Mission will be integrated into the implementation 
where applicable. During the implementation phase of the strategy, systematic 
coordination with ongoing national development projects, legislative reforms and studies 
will be ensured.

Costs and projected savings
According to a revised estimate made in connection with the formulation of the roadmap, the 
implementation of the strategy in the first five-year period 2026–2031 requires a total investment of 
approximately €140 million. Measure-specific cost estimates are presented in Appendix 1. The 
implementation of the strategy promotes effective cancer prevention, early detection and 
treatment. The implementation of the cancer strategy as a whole is a savings and growth measure. 
Cost savings are expected to total €298 million during the strategy period, and they are distributed 
by main objective as follows:
Strengthening inclusion and developing people-oriented services
In terms of strengthening inclusion and developing people-oriented services, cost 
savings are expected to total €2 million. These consist of measures under sub-objective 
1.3, which focus on developing health literacy, promoting participation in vaccinations 
and screenings, and early treatment seeking.
Reducing the burden of cancer through prevention and early detection
The estimated savings in cancer prevention, totaling €157 million, are estimated to arise from 
sub-objective 2.1, whose measures reduce smoking and alcohol consumption by preventing a 
significant number of cancer cases. The savings have been calculated using a scenario in which 
smoking and alcohol consumption decrease by 10% per year for ten years as a result of the 
measures and in which the growth in the proportion of overweight people stops.
Ensuring equal and effective cancer treatment
The development of equal and effective cancer care is expected to bring savings, but their 
assessment, due to the complexity of care pathways and service systems and the 
development of operating models, requires the development and integration of the 
knowledge base presented in the strategy. These assessments will be carried out as the 
implementation progresses. The savings anticipated in the strategy for this main objective (19 
M€) have been estimated to arise from the development of palliative care, based on 
calculations presented in previous reports and studies.88.
